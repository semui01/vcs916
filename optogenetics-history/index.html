From an alga’s eyespot to a switch for the brain

In 1866 a St Petersburg botanist watched green algae swim toward light. In 2026 the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to the people who found the protein behind that behaviour and turned it into a way to switch neurons on and off with light. This is how that happened.

Chapter 3 · How it works

Switch lab: drive a neuron with light

A simplified neuron (leaky integrate-and-fire) expressing one opsin at a time. Channelrhodopsins are ion channels that light opens directly, so ions flow down their gradient within milliseconds. Pumps such as halorhodopsin move one ion per photon. Change the opsin, the pulse rate and the pulse width, and watch how faithfully the cell follows. Chapter 4 · 1971–2007

Before the switch: what earlier methods lacked

The Nobel Committee lists what a practical light actuator needed: genetic targeting, a single compact protein, no added chemicals, millisecond speed and good tolerance by the cell. Each earlier approach met some of these. ChR2 was the first to meet all of them.

Chapter 5 · 2007 onward

From reading the brain to writing to it

With optical fibres, light could reach deep structures in freely moving animals. Turning specific cells or projections on and off showed which ones are sufficient or necessary for a behaviour. Chapter 6 · Clinical frontier

Making blind retinas see light again

In retinitis pigmentosa the rods and cones die, but the retinal ganglion cells that send signals to the brain survive. If those cells carry a channelrhodopsin, they can respond to light directly. This was Pan’s goal from the start, and it is still the most advanced clinical use of optogenetics.

Chapter 7 · Who invented it?

The answer depends on the rules you use

The 2016 STAT investigation asked what “inventing” means in science. Academics count the first peer-reviewed paper. Patent law counts when an idea was conceived and first shown to work. Prizes follow reputation, which grows on itself. The 2026 Nobel went to the discovery of the channel itself and to its first use as a neuronal switch.

Shown: four major prizes for optogenetics. Pan received none of them. His patent on restoring vision with channelrhodopsin was licensed by RetroSense, whose 2016 trial was the first use of optogenetics in humans.
